## The findings I did not expect

Five things I did not know before opening the files, each computed in this session
from the two registered raw CSVs:

1. **There is no administrative censoring in the extract.** Missing containment
   time is zero rows in both files. The brief's censoring mechanism, fires still
   burning at the end of the record, has no instances. (S2)
2. **Containment times are administratively rounded and report times are not.**
   25.6 per cent of containments land exactly on the hour, 38.4 per cent on the
   hour or half hour, against report minutes that are effectively uniform. A
   discrete-time hazard binned in fire-hours will show spikes on its own bin
   edges that are a data-entry convention. (S1)
3. **Duration and final size are nearly the same variable.** No fire that reached
   10 ha lasted under 3.67 h, and all 78 of them were still burning at hour three.
   Surviving to hour three lifts the base rate from 3.9 to 12.2 per cent using no
   covariate at all. (S3, D1)
4. **The known Uiseong duplicate is in the statistics file too**, at ranks one and
   two of the size distribution, which is the worst place for it. The registry
   attaches it only to the state history file. No exact-duplicate check finds it,
   because the two rows carry different areas. (D8)
5. **The two committed files cannot be joined**, and they carry different clocks
   (report-to-containment, median 2.00 h, against suppression-start-to-containment,
   median 0.87 h). The size lives in one file and the suppression clock in the
   other. (S4, D3)

Item 4 is a registry correction and I am flagging it for A1 rather than editing
`REGISTRY.yaml`, which I do not own.

## Answer to the question the brief put to me

The brief says the neural extreme-value model is to be considered **only if** I
confirm the sample of large fires is sufficient. **I do not confirm it.** There
are 78 exceedances above 10 ha and 25 above 100 ha in four years, the largest two
observations are 73.6 per cent of all area burned and are plausibly one fire
entered twice, and a covariate-dependent generalized Pareto already carries
roughly a dozen free parameters against that sample. The answer is no, and it is
not close.
