# Summary for a non-specialist reader (kcf-idea-forecast)

**The problem.** Our evacuation tool draws a safety distance around a fire: how far the fire could get in the next five hours. A distance of 3.8 km held on 28 large California fires. On 25 March 2025 in Gyeongbuk the fire travelled up to 40 km in five hours, and nothing we tried earlier (how fast the fire had been moving, or gusts at weather stations) warned of it.

**The idea.** In strong wind a fire spreads partly by burning embers that blow ahead and start new fires. An ember leaving a ridge top falls at a few metres per second while the wind carries it sideways. If the wind is strong enough, the ember's path is flatter than the hillside falling away beneath it. It then cannot land on the downwind side of the ridge at all. It crosses the valley and lights the next hill. The fire no longer has to burn down one side and up the other; it hops from ridge to ridge. The condition is one line of arithmetic: wind speed × steepness of the downwind slope must exceed the ember's fall speed. Above that wind, the fire's speed should rise faster than the usual rule of thumb (a fire moves at about one-tenth of the wind speed) predicts.

**Is it new?** Fire scientists have said in words that spot fires let a fire skip slow downhill stretches, and a 2021 wind-tunnel study showed it on a model hill. We could not find the condition or the speed formula written down anywhere, after searching eight ways and reading the three closest papers.

**The test.** Before looking at any outcome we wrote down the data, the measure and the pass marks. Every number in the formula came from published measurements; none was adjusted to fit a fire. Korean data were kept for the final check only.

**What happened.**
- In California the test could not run. The global weather record we used never shows a ridge-height wind strong enough to cross the threshold at any of the 28 fires, so the idea and the rule of thumb predict the same thing there.
- In Gyeongbuk the idea failed. It raised the predicted fire speed by at most 7%. The fire went up to 2.7 times farther than predicted. The idea missed the same 8 of 23 hours as the plain rule of thumb.
- Two reasons stand out. The hills are gentler than we assumed when measured on a 90 m map: a typical downwind slope would need a 30 m/s wind. And each hop costs time while the new fire gets going; with a five-minute delay, hopping barely beats ordinary spread.

**Verdict: new, but not testable with this data.** We do not claim the idea is right. On the one day it was meant to explain, it explained nothing.

**One useful by-product.** The plain one-tenth-of-wind rule, with no tuning, missed 8 of 23 hours on 25 March where our calibrated 3.8 km distance missed 13, and at 10–11 in the morning, when the fire still looked quiet, it already called for 12–14 km. That is one day and assumes a perfect wind forecast, so it is a lead to check, not a result.

**What a proper test needs.** Wind measured at ridge height, a finer terrain map, and fire arrival times fine enough to see whether the far hillside really lights before the near one.
