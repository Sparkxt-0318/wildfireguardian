## 1. What pyrogeography is

**Definition and origin.** Pyrogeography is the study of the distribution, drivers and consequences of fire across space and time, uniting biological, atmospheric and social perspectives. The programmatic statement is Bowman et al. (2009, *Science*, "Fire in the Earth System"), written by the NCEAS/KITP Pyrogeography and Climate Change working group; the human side is developed in Bowman et al. (2011, *J. Biogeography*, "The human dimension of fire regimes on Earth") and the management framing in Bowman, O'Brien & Goldammer (2013, *Annu. Rev. Environ. Resour.*), which describes the field as combining "biology, atmospheric sciences, social sciences" to understand fire at broad scales [abstract].

**Fire regime vs. fire event.** A *fire event* is one fire; a *fire regime* is the statistical character of fire in a place over decades — frequency, size, intensity, season, type (surface / crown / ground) and extent. Archibald et al. (2013, *PNAS*) formalise five regime characteristics — **size, frequency, intensity, season and extent** — and show by Bayesian clustering that only certain combinations occur, yielding five global syndromes, or **pyromes**: four distinguish crown-, litter- and grass-fuelled fire, and a fifth "represents human-engineered modifications to fire characteristics" [opened, abstract text]. WildfireGuardian's six fires are *events* drawn from one pyrome (temperate conifer litter/crown fire under a human ignition regime); its LOFO-CV asks whether a model trained on five events of that regime generalises to a sixth, which is a within-regime question, not a regime-level claim.

**The "switches".** Bradstock (2010, *Global Ecol. Biogeogr.*) models a fire regime as the product of four switches that must all be "on": **biomass growth (B)**, **availability to burn / dryness (A)**, **fire weather / spread conditions (S)** and **ignition (I)**; the regime is limited by whichever switch flips least often [abstract]. Krawchuk & Moritz (2011, *Ecology*) test the related **varying-constraints hypothesis** globally (2001–2007, monthly): where fuel is always present (productive biomes) fuel *moisture* is the binding constraint; where fuel is scarce or variable (deserts, grasslands) antecedent fuel *production* binds instead [opened, PDF]. Krawchuk et al. (2009, *PLoS ONE*, "Global pyrogeography") and Moritz et al. (2012, *Ecosphere*) build the first statistical global fire-probability models at ~100 km / 0.5° resolution [abstract]. The lesson for a per-cell model is that the *relative* weight of fuel, dryness, wind and ignition is itself geographically conditional — a model fitted in one switch-regime need not transfer (cf. the project's zero-shot US collapse noted in `docs/global_portability.md`).

**Regimes under climate change.** Jones et al. (2022, *Rev. Geophys.*, 76 pp.) review regional trends: fire *weather* is intensifying almost everywhere, but *burned area* trends are mediated by vegetation, ignition, suppression and land use, so global burned area has fallen while extreme events rise [abstract]. Cunningham, Williamson & Bowman (2024, *Nat. Ecol. Evol.*) find the frequency of the most extreme fire events (≥99.99th percentile of daily FRP) rose 2.2-fold from 2003 to 2023, concentrated in boreal and temperate conifer biomes, with the six most extreme years all in the last seven [abstract]. Balch et al. (2022, *Nature*) show the night-time barrier to fire (low VPD) is weakening [abstract]. Korea's temperate-conifer, human-ignited regime sits squarely in the biome class these papers flag.

## 2. East Asian and Korean pyrogeography

### 2.1 The spring season and the numbers behind it

Korea's fire season is a **late-winter–spring** season, not a summer one. Kim, Kim, Lee & Im (2025, *Natural Hazards* 121: 9801–9821) analyse 1991–2020: an average of **451 fires/yr**, rising **5.82 fires/yr**; **80.7 %** of annual burned area falls in **April–May**; the fire season of 2006–2020 was **25 days longer** than 1991–2005; large-fire risk is concentrating in Gangwon and Gyeongbuk [opened, abstract via RePEc]. The Korea Forest Service's own summary (KFS, International Cooperation Division, deck dated 2025-11-06, distributed by the World Bank) gives **440 cases / 857 ha per year in the 2010s → 520 cases / 6,720 ha per year in 2020–2024**, and sets the statutory **spring precaution period at 1 Feb–15 May** (autumn 1 Nov–15 Dec) [opened, PDF]. KFS's public statistics page (산불발생현황, read 2026-09-04) reports a **2016–2025 ten-year mean of 536.8 fires and 3,559.64 ha per year**, and for 2025 alone 756 fires [opened]. Park, Suh & Baek (2025, *Forests* 16: 1476) analyse 905 fires ≥ 5 ha over 1980–2024 and find burned area in 2020–2024 (29,905 ha) was **sevenfold** 2015–2019; each +1 m s⁻¹ of peak wind added ~8.5 ha and each +1 % RH removed ~3 ha of burned area; high conifer and mature-stand fractions enlarged fires while broadleaf prevalence restrained them [abstract].

⚠ *Scope discrepancy to carry, not resolve:* the KFS statistics page's 2025 annual total (24,797.16 ha, as read 2026-09-04) is far below the KFS 2025-04-18 provisional damage figure of ~104,000 ha for the March fires alone (§2.4). The two are different KFS products (산불피해대장 forest-fire ledger vs. the joint central–local damage survey); which one the project cites must always say so.

### 2.2 Yangganjipung (양간지풍) — the east-coast foehn

Kim, Kwak & Kim (2021, *J. Korean Soc. Hazard Mitig.* 21(4): 39–48) define Yangganjipung as the **westerly generated in March–April when a low sits south and a high north of the peninsula; crossing the Taebaek range it arrives hot, dry and strong** on the Yeongdong coast; they name the April 2000 East Coast fire, April 2005 Yangyang fire and April 2019 Goseong fire as its products, note that **8 of the 13 large fires (≥100 ha or ≥24 h) of 2010–2019 were on the Gangwon east coast**, and that Gangwon's ten-year mean damage (530.91 ha) was 3.5× Gyeongbuk's (154.21 ha) [opened, PDF]. The meteorology is downslope-windstorm dynamics: Lee et al. (2020, *Atmosphere* 11: 431) simulate the 8 April 2012 event with WRF and attribute the Yangyang windstorm to a **hydraulic jump** with lee-slope isentrope descent, gap winds at Jangjeon and banded PBL structure at Uljin [abstract]; Shin et al. (2022, *JGR Atmospheres* 127) classify 1979–2019 events by self-organising map into three synoptic types — the **spring south-high/north-low type shows the strongest adiabatic warming and is "likely to have a huge impact on the spread of wildfires"**, with mountain-wave breaking at critical levels [abstract]. Won et al. (2010) already note "the sea wind and Föhn create hazardous forest fire conditions" [opened]. The 2023 Gangneung fire (11 April 2023; KFS attributed it to a wind-snapped tree on a power line; gusts to 30 m s⁻¹ grounded helicopters; >379 ha; one death — *Korea Times*, 2023-04-11) is the most recent Yangganjipung case in the project's dataset [abstract/press]. <!-- forbidden-ok: 154 -->

### 2.3 Fuel: *Pinus densiflora* and the human ignition regime

Korea is ~63 % forested (Park et al. 2025) and the east-coast fire fuel is dominated by Japanese red pine. Baek, Lim & Kim (2022, *Forests* 13: 2185) map the Uljin 2022 fire at 5-day Sentinel-2 cadence and find the core expanded very rapidly in the first days, then *intensified* rather than expanded; **coniferous stands showed ~3× the severity of deciduous stands**, yet the fire "did not expand selectively by forest type" — severity, not perimeter, tracked fuel [abstract]. KFS's public Q&A on spotting (비화, 산불 Q&A 03-05) states that conifers hold **1.4× the heat energy and burn 2.4× longer** than broadleaves, and that pine crown-fire embers (branches, cones, bark) lifted by updraughts can travel **up to ~2 km** (35 km was reported in Australia in 2009) [opened, PDF]. Crown-fuel allometry for *P. densiflora* is being built now (Song et al. 2026, *Forests* 17: 549 [abstract]).

Ignition is almost entirely human. KFS (2025-11-06 deck; scope 2015–2024): **accidental fire by mountain visitors 37 %, incineration (agricultural/waste burning) 27 %, cigarettes 7 %, building fires 7 %, careless ash 5 %, workplace 5 %, tomb visitors 4 %, other 8 %** [opened]. Lim & Chae (2026, *Fire* 9: 147; scope 2004–2023) find visitor ignitions 35.5 %, field/ridge burning 13.3 %, waste burning 12.1 %, cigarettes 6.6 %, and derive Canadian-FWI thresholds of **4.39 (≥1 fire/day) and 6.84 (≥2 fires/day)**; ignitions cluster in age-class 4–5 conifer stands and, in metropolitan fringes, within 150 m of buildings [abstract]. Won et al. (2010) record that "no fires were caused by natural phenomena according to the official statistics collected since 2000" [opened]. Heo et al. (2026, *Forests* 17: 182) model March 2025 ignition susceptibility: probability rises under **>25 °C, <25 % RH, >6 m s⁻¹**, near graves, trails and forest roads, and in contiguous conifer stands ≥30 ha [abstract]. Lee, Ahn & Im (2026, *Forests* 17: 281) add distance to farmland and roads to the east-coast model [abstract]. Ji, Lee & Park (2026, *Forests* 17: 934) report Gangneung alone carries ~56 % of Gangwon's decade fire damage; hikers' negligence led ignitions (30.8 %) [abstract].

### 2.5 Fire-danger forecasting: KFS/NIFoS 산불위험예보

The Korean Forest Fire Danger Rating System (KFFDRS; now 국가산불위험예보시스템, forestfire.nifos.go.kr) is described by Won, Lee, Lee & Ohga (2010, *J. Fac. Agric. Kyushu Univ.* 55(2): 221–229): three 10-scale indices — a **Daily Weather Index (DWI)** from eight regional logistic regressions on *effective humidity* (r = 0.7 weighting of prior days), maximum temperature and wind; a **Fuel Model Index (FMI)** and a **Topography Model Index (TMI)** derived from 126 fires (1997–2001). Effective humidity was significant everywhere; maximum temperature in Gangwon, Gyeonggi, Gyeongbuk, Jeonbuk; **mean wind speed was significant only in Gyeongbuk** [opened]. The live site (read 2026-09-04) publishes the index by 시군구 at half-hourly stamps with a separate **대형산불위험예보** and **동해안산불위험정보** product [opened]. Lim & Chae (2025, *Forests* 16: 1058) test the Canadian FWI against Korean occurrence [abstract]; Lee et al. (2025, *Sci. Rep.*, Gangwon daily model) find RH, precipitation and temperature dominate SHAP importance (Extra-Trees AUC 0.839) [abstract]. Note the pattern across all of these: **occurrence models in Korea are humidity-led and wind is a secondary or regional term — the opposite of spread models, where wind leads.** The project's `spread_v2` is a spread model and should not be benchmarked against occurrence indices.

## 3. Spread drivers at 375–500 m and 3–12 h

WildfireGuardian's label is "cell ignites by the **next satellite overpass**" (gaps 3–12 h, gated at 90 min) on a **500 m** hazard grid with **hourly ERA5** weather at **0.25°** (Hersbach et al. 2020, *QJRMS*: ~31 km grid) [abstract]. At that scale the literature says:

- **Wind.** Cruz & Alexander (2019, *Ann. For. Sci.*) show from 118 high-intensity wildfires that forward ROS in conifer forests, dry eucalypt and shrubland is **≈ 10 % of the 10-m open wind speed** (same units), with errors <50 % under dry, windy conditions; the rule does *not* hold in grass [abstract]. Cruz & Alexander (2013, *Environ. Model. Softw.* 47: 16–28) compile 1,278 ROS observations: only **3 % of model predictions were exact**, mean errors ran 20–310 %, and **±35 %** is proposed as the adequacy standard [abstract]. Any per-cell spread model is working inside that noise floor.
- **Slope and aspect.** Sharples (2009, *IJWF* 18: 737–754) reviews mountain meteorology for fire: terrain channelling, lee-slope eddies, foehn and diurnal slope flows make near-surface wind highly variable in rugged terrain [abstract]. The Australian rule that ROS doubles per 10° of upslope (McArthur) is questioned by Sullivan et al. (2014, *Environ. Model. Softw.*), who find it over-estimates relative to Rothermel's slope term **[UNVERIFIED — page not opened]**. Rothermel (1972, USDA INT-115) remains the surface-fire ROS model behind US systems [abstract]; Sullivan's three-part 2009 *IJWF* review (18: 349–403) is the standard map of physical, empirical and simulation models [abstract]. The project's own **DEM defect** (Uljin DEM filled to −497 m over the sea, 49 % of the raster; `docs/dem_defect_2026-08-02.md`) is a reminder that slope features are only as good as the elevation source.
- **Fuel moisture, VPD, days since rain.** Sedano & Randerson (2014, *Biogeosciences* 11: 3739) link VPD anomalies to ignition, growth and burned area in Alaska (R² = 0.45 regionally) [abstract]; Balch et al. (2022) make VPD the night-time gate [abstract]. WWA's 2025 Korea attribution used HDWI = max wind × max VPD [opened]. The project already carries `vpd_kpa`, `rh_pct`, `days_since_rain`, `precip_24h_mm`.
- **Crown fire and spotting.** Cruz & Alexander (2010, *IJWF* 19: 377–398) document a systematic *under*-prediction bias of crowning onset and crown-fire ROS in operational US systems [abstract]; crown ROS is several times surface ROS. KFS's ~2 km pine-ember ceiling (§2.3) and the 2025 51-km run (§2.4) bracket the spotting scale: **at 500 m cells, a 2 km spot is four cells beyond the front; the project's far-band (>3 km) AUC exists precisely for this** (`MODEL_CARD.md`).
- **What ERA5 cannot resolve.** Sjöström, Vermina Plathner & Granström (2025, *Sci. Rep.*) find ERA5-derived FWI **~25 % lower** than station-observed FWI whenever FWI > 5 in Sweden [abstract]; Kanwal & Song (2026, *Fire* 9: 283) conclude ERA5's ~27 km grid "cannot resolve" terrain-channelled local spread even when synoptic direction is right [abstract]; a search-level synthesis puts ERA5 near-surface wind **30–50 % low in mountains** **[UNVERIFIED as a single citation]**. This is exactly the project's withdrawn "44×" claim and its Session 10–13 finding: **ERA5 wind direction was 83.5° off the observed spread azimuth (90° = no relation), slope explained direction only where steep (50.8° vs 89.4°), and none of three direction features raised AUC** (`docs/direction_findings.md`). The literature says this is a resolution result, not a physics result — and the project already says the same.

## 4. Detection and burned-area products, and how "spread" becomes a label

- **FIRMS VIIRS/MODIS active fire.** Schroeder et al. (2014, *RSE* 143: 85–96): VIIRS 375 m contextual algorithm, ~1.2 % commission for nominal-confidence detections, more coherent perimeters than MODIS 1 km [abstract]. NASA FIRMS FAQ (read 2026-09-04): NRT data within **~3 h** of overpass (best effort); VIIRS gives **3–4 observations/day at mid-latitudes**; a detection marks the **pixel centre**, not the fire edge; clouds, smoke and dense canopy suppress detection [opened]. Fu et al. (2020, *Remote Sens.* 12: 2870, Northeast Asia): **65 % of forest fire pixels seen by VIIRS were missed by MODIS** [abstract] — the project's choice of VIIRS over MODIS is supported for Korean forest.
- **Geostationary.** GK2A AMI images the Korean peninsula every 2 min at 2 km (IR). Jang et al. (2019, *Remote Sens.* 11: 271) detect Korean fires from Himawari-8 with POD ~93 % and more than half within 10 min [abstract]; Kang et al. (2022, *GISci. Remote Sens.* 59: 2019–2035) cut latency further with deep learning [abstract]; Sung et al. (2025, *KJRS* 41(3): 565–580) report for March 2025 a mean detection delay of **12.9 min (AMI LightGBM) vs 318.1 min (VIIRS)** [abstract; number from publisher snippet]. The project's own measurement (`docs/detection_floor.md` §10) agrees on the ordering — GK2A beats FIRMS in two of three fires — but adds that **the human report beat both** (GK2A 22–64 min behind the 119/KFS report), which is why the manual trigger is primary.
- **Burned area.** Giglio et al. (2018, *RSE* 217: 72–85): MCD64A1 C6 at 500 m, better small-burn detection, "modest" burn-date improvement [abstract]. Giglio et al. (2025, *RSE*): global Landsat validation gives **VNP64A1 36.7 % commission / 72.4 % omission, MCD64A1 34.2 % / 71.5 %; only 71–72 % of burn dates fall within ±2 days** [abstract]. That date error is the same order as the project's label interval. Sentinel-2 dNBR (Key & Benson 2006, FIREMON RMRS-GTR-164; MTBS, Eidenshink et al. 2007, *Fire Ecol.* 3: 3–21) is the severity standard [abstract]; for Uljin, Kim, Lee, Park & Im (2022, *KJRS* 38(5): 765–779) find dNBR best (OA 0.91, F1 0.84, Sentinel-2A), with the burn **clearer weeks after the fire than immediately** [opened]. Sentinel-2 is a post-hoc perimeter source, not a progression source.
- **How progression datasets define "spread".** *Next Day Wildfire Spread* (Huot et al. 2022, *IEEE TGRS* 60: 1–13): 18,545 fire events, 64 km squares at **1 km**, MOD14A1 fire mask at *t* and *t*+1 day [opened, arXiv PDF]. *WildfireSpreadTS* (Gerard, Zhao & Sullivan 2023, NeurIPS D&B): 607 US fires, 13,607 daily images, label = **next day's binarised VNP14IMG active-fire mask**, all detections in a 24 h window collapsed into one mask, ~0.1 % positives, labels "noisy … due to smoke, clouds, and inaccuracies in the active fire detection"; events selected from GlobFire and 948 of 1,555 discarded for having no VIIRS detections [opened, PDF]. WSTS+ (2025) doubles the years and reports AP [abstract]. *FIRED* (Balch et al. 2020, *Remote Sens.* 12: 3498): MCD64A1 pixels clustered with an **11-day / 5-pixel (2,315 m)** window, tuned to MTBS perimeters [abstract]. *GlobFire* (Artés et al. 2019, *Sci. Data*): burnt patches touching in space and within **5 days** are one event [opened]. **Every one of these labels "spread" as a change in a satellite mask between two fixed times; the project's "by next overpass" label is the same family at a variable, shorter interval** — which is why `MODEL_CARD.md` refuses cross-benchmark comparison.

## 5. Evacuation-relevant pyrogeography

- **Arrival time and trigger buffers.** Cova, Dennison, Kim & Moritz (2005, *Trans. GIS* 9: 603–617) invert the question: given an evacuation time, run fire spread backwards from the community to draw a **trigger buffer** — when fire crosses it, evacuate [abstract]. Li, Cova & Dennison (2015, *Comput. Environ. Urban Syst.* 54: 56–67) take this to the **household level**, staging warnings per house with trigger modelling in Julian, CA [abstract] — the closest published analogue to WildfireGuardian's sampled-origin routing. Mitchell, Gwynne, Ronchi, Kalogeropoulos & Rein (2023, *Safety Sci.* 157: 105914, PERIL) define trigger *perimeters* where fire-travel time to the community equals required evacuation time with a safety factor, and report surveyed evacuation times averaging 31 min (0–148) against 105 min (18–150) observed at assembly points [abstract].
- **Rate-of-spread distributions.** Grass: Cheney, Gould & Catchpole (1998, *IJWF* 8: 1–13) predict ROS from 10-m wind, dead-fuel moisture and curing with a regime change at 5 km h⁻¹ wind [abstract]; grass fires routinely exceed forest ROS. Conifer forest: the 10 % rule (Cruz & Alexander 2019) gives ~2.5 km h⁻¹ at 25 km h⁻¹ wind; the Korean 2025 record of **8.2 km h⁻¹** (KFS, §2.4) at ~25–27 m s⁻¹ gusts (≈ 90–97 km h⁻¹) is consistent with that rule to within its stated error. For a pedestrian at 1.0–1.4 m s⁻¹ (3.6–5 km h⁻¹), **a Yangganjipung-driven pine crown fire out-runs walking**, and embers pre-empt it by up to 2 km (KFS Q&A).
- **WUI.** Radeloff et al. (2005, *Ecol. Appl.* 15: 799–805) define the WUI as where houses meet or intermingle with wildland vegetation, split into *intermix* and *interface* by housing density and vegetation cover; Radeloff et al. (2018, *PNAS* 115: 3314–3319) show the US WUI grew ~41 % in area 1990–2010 [abstract]. **The numeric thresholds usually quoted (≥6.17 housing units km⁻², ≥50 % vegetation for intermix, 2.4 km to a ≥5 km² vegetated block for interface) could not be opened by the routine and are [UNVERIFIED] here.** Korean WUI work has started (Ji et al. 2026; Park et al. 2025; Lim & Chae 2026) but no Korean WUI census exists; `WUI_BUILDINGS_AS_FUEL.md` records why buildings-as-fuel is post-finals.
- **"Time until a road is cut."** None of the above literature gives a Korean statistic. The translation is arithmetic: **road-cut time = (distance from front to road along the spread axis) / ROS**, minus spotting lead. At 8.2 km h⁻¹ a road 2 km ahead is cut in ~15 min; at the 10 %-rule 2.5 km h⁻¹ in ~48 min. The project's time-expanded router already consumes this as the `P(ignite)` field per step; what the literature adds is the **spread of ROS** — Cruz & Alexander's ±35 % adequacy band and their 20–310 % observed mean errors — which is the uncertainty a judge will ask the router to survive (`docs/forecast_robustness.md` measured breakage at 125–530 m of spatial error and ~122 m of dilation).

## 6. What this means for WildfireGuardian

**Insights (each with its source).**

1. **The withdrawn "44×" claim is the literature's result too.** ERA5 at 0.25° describes synoptic context, not terrain-channelled spread wind (Kanwal & Song 2026; Sharples 2009; Sjöström et al. 2025 for the FWI bias). Session 10–13's 83.5° misalignment is what those papers predict. Say it as "not resolvable at this resolution", never as "wind does not matter".
2. **Korea is a spring, human-ignition, pine-litter/crown pyrome with a Yangganjipung amplifier.** Six events in one regime is a within-pyrome test (Archibald et al. 2013); the zero-shot US collapse is the expected between-pyrome failure (Krawchuk & Moritz 2011), not a bug.
3. **Occurrence and spread are different problems with different dominant switches.** KFDRS/DWI is humidity-led and wind matters only regionally (Won et al. 2010; Lee et al. 2025); spread is wind-led (Cruz & Alexander 2019). Do not let a judge compare `spread_v2` with 산불위험예보.
4. **The label family is shared with WSTS/NDWS but the interval is not.** WSTS collapses 24 h of VNP14IMG into one mask and calls its labels noisy (Gerard et al. 2023); the project's 3–12 h overpass label is the same object at finer, variable cadence, so the model card's refusal to compare AUC to AP is correct and citable.
5. **Burn-date error in the reference products is of the order of the label interval.** Only 71–72 % of MCD64A1/VNP64A1 burn dates are within ±2 days (Giglio et al. 2025); the project's IoU against a cumulative envelope (~0.40 cumulative, ~0.07 new-ring) should be read against that ceiling.
6. **Spotting sets a physical scale the grid can express.** KFS's ~2 km pine-ember reach is four 500 m cells; the 2025 51 km/12 h run is far beyond any local term. The far-band (>3 km) AUC is the right diagnostic; a *downwind* far-band split is the natural next question (B2 below).
7. **The 8.2 km h⁻¹ record makes the "cell ignites by next overpass" horizon concrete.** 3 h → ~25 km; 12 h → ~98 km. `docs/horizon_grounding.md` should carry this arithmetic with the KFS/MBC attribution.
