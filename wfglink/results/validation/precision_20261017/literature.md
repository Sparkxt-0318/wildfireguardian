## nist_tn2093

Citation: Bouvet N, Link ED, Fink SA (2020) Development of a New Approach to Characterize Firebrand Showers During Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) Fires: a Step Towards High-Fidelity Measurements in Three Dimensions. NIST Technical Note 2093, National Institute of Standards and Technology, Gaithersburg MD, May 2020, 52 pages. https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.TN.2093 (PDF: https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/TechnicalNotes/NIST.TN.2093.pdf). The device is called the "emberometer" in the text (Sec. 1.1, 4.1, 4.2.3, 6); the word does not appear in the title.

Relevant methods:

- Camera hardware and geometry (Sec. 2.2.1, pp. 12 to 13, Fig. 5; Table 1 last row p. 4; Table 2 last row p. 7): Four consumer-grade compact cameras (Sony DSC-RX10 M3, about 20.1 Mpx) mounted at both ends of two horizontal rails (2.03 m long) whose upper faces are at 0.356 m and 1.880 m height, on a mobile stand so the system can be moved without performing additional calibrations. Cameras aimed with tripod heads so image centers closely coincide with a target point T (0 m, 1.041 m, 0 m) on which focus is set; each camera carries a collimated laser diode and all beams intersect at T, which is also used at the start of each experiment to detect unwanted changes of camera orientation. Minimum focal length (f = 8.8 mm), largest aperture (f/2.4). Control volume is the intersection of the fields of view, corrected for small volumes not accounted for during spatial calibration, estimated at about 1.91 m3 (Table 1 rounds it to 2 m3). Working distance about 1.2 m to 2.8 m (Table 2). No illumination is used; tracers are glowing firebrands (Table 1).
- Acquisition and synchronization (Sec. 2.2.2, pp. 13 to 14; Fig. 22 caption p. 30): HD video (1920x1080 px) at a nominal 120 fps. Near-field triggering by IR remote receivers; long-range triggering via a custom RF control hub (433.92 MHz) that also triggers a sync laser. Streams are synchronized post-acquisition on the disappearance of a small laser dot, manually operated and visible from all cameras. Synchronization verified by imaging a millisecond-precision digital counter; error found to be within the time lapse between two consecutive frames, up to the highest available frame rate (960 fps). The frame rate actually used for tracking in the Sec. 4 shower is not stated; Fig. 22 says a 30-timestep window is about 1 s, which implies (my inference) roughly 30 timesteps per second after temporal downsampling, not 120.
- Preprocessing pipeline (Sec. 2.2.3, pp. 14 to 15, Fig. 6): Frames are extracted unaltered from video (32-bit color) with commercial software, then synchronized and converted to 8-bit grayscale in an in-house MATLAB code, with optional temporal downsampling to reduce tracking cost when motion is too highly resolved. Grayscale sequences plus spatial calibration go to OpenPTV; an in-house FORTRAN code builds per-particle 'ID I' files (frame number, time step, 3D object coordinates, 2D coordinates in all four views, velocity from previous and current positions, distance D and local resolution R*). Color sequences are used later for sizing.
- Particle detection (Sec. 2.3.1 p. 15; Sec. 2.3.3 pp. 16 to 17; Sec. 2.4.1 p. 19; Table A1 p. 44): Firebrand 2D image coordinates are found with a pixel-intensity-weighted centroid operator inside OpenPTV. The text gives no detection threshold values, no background subtraction or background model, and no size limits for detection. The tracking-efficiency monitoring described below is said to provide, 'to some extent', guidance for choosing detection and tracking parameters. The uncertainty table assumes uniform pixel intensity across silhouettes (no bias in centroid detection). For sizing only, particle boundaries are found by a multi-step boundary detection that follows an unsupervised threshold selection method (Otsu 1979, ref. [56]) and needs no user input.
- Spatial calibration (Sec. 2.3.2, p. 16, Fig. 7; Table A1 p. 44): 3D V-shaped target with 76 dots of known 3D coordinates, all visible from the four viewpoints, processed by spatial resection to get internal and external orientation of each camera. A verification test is systematically performed after calibration by re-processing the original target images and comparing known and measured target point positions; the expanded uncertainty in position is typically on the order of plus or minus 1.5 mm in all directions. Because the verification re-uses the calibration target images, this is an in-sample residual, not an independent test on separate points.
- Multiview correspondence and 3D reconstruction (Sec. 2.1 pp. 11 to 12, Fig. 4; Sec. 2.3.1 p. 15): Correspondence across views by epipolar line intersection: the search for a match in view 2 is restricted to a narrow band around the epipolar line, limited in length by the known depth of the object space and with a transverse tolerance; intersecting the search bands from the 3rd and 4th views establishes the correspondence unambiguously, and 3D coordinates are computed, for instance by least-squares adjustment of the collinearity equations. The text states that at least two images from calibrated devices with different orientations are needed to recover 3D coordinates.
- Spatiotemporal tracking (Sec. 2.3.1 p. 15; Sec. 4.2.1 p. 28, Fig. 20): Tracking by spatio-temporal particle matching using both image-space and object-space information (OpenPTV, following Maas et al. and Willneff). No tracking parameter values (search radii, acceleration limits) are given in the text. Only particles inside the control volume are tracked; firebrands visible in camera 4 near the generator exit that have not yet entered the control volume are not tracked.
- Robustness from redundant views (Sec. 4.2.1, p. 28, Fig. 20): The authors argue the quad-view setup suits firebrands because visibility can vary strongly with imaging angle (for example background interference). Particle #145 has an incomplete trajectory in the image plane of camera 1, but its 3D trajectory is still reconstructed because the other image planes have the information.
- Tracking-efficiency monitoring (Sec. 2.3.3 pp. 16 to 17, Fig. 8, footnote 2): Qualitative check: at each timestep count all particles located in 3D inside a test volume chosen well inside the control volume (X -600 to 600 mm, Y 600 to 1400 mm, Z -300 to 300 mm for the Sec. 4 case), split into 'tracked' (identified before and after the current timestep), 'new' (identified for the first time in the current timestep) and 'lost' (not identified beyond the current timestep). A close match between total and tracked counts indicates good tracking efficiency. The test volume is kept well within the control volume to avoid counting appearance and disappearance events near the control-volume boundaries. Described as providing, to some extent, guidance for choosing detection and tracking parameters. New and lost events are described as seldom and usually associated with the non-ideal trajectory types below.
- Trajectory taxonomy and spurious-trajectory rejection (Sec. 2.3.3, p. 17): Three non-ideal trajectory types: (1) spurious: unwanted, lasting very few time steps with very small averaged displacement, usually due to a secondary tracked object (examples given: a landed firebrand, a bright element in the background); typically filtered out prior to any tracking or sizing analysis with an operator based on the mean particle displacement, but it may remain on rare occasions if the operator is set to include very slow (small) firebrands; (2) incomplete or shortened: valid trajectory with late start or early end inside the measurement volume, missing steps possibly due to lack of visibility, processed like full trajectories; (3) broken: valid portion of a trajectory for which at least one sister portion can be found in the measurement volume, correspondences unknown a priori. The threshold value of the mean-displacement operator is NOT reported, the text does not say whether the displacement is measured in 3D object space or in image space (it is applied to the reconstructed 3D trajectories, so object space is the likely reading, but that is an inference), and no separate minimum-length rule is stated.
- Tradeoff for slow genuine firebrands (Fig. 21 caption p. 30; Sec. 4.2.1 p. 29; Sec. 2.3.3 p. 17): Fig. 21b shows a reconstructed 'slow' firebrand trajectory, presented by the authors as an example of the low-velocity ember dynamics the system resolves, that does not appear in the cumulative plot of Fig. 21a because it is filtered out by the mean displacement operator. So the displacement filter removed a trajectory the authors treat as a real slow ember from the analysed set. Sec. 2.3.3 also acknowledges the tradeoff: a spurious trajectory may remain if the operator is set to include very slow (small) firebrands. Low-velocity motion (below 0.4 m/s) of small, air-draft and lift-force sensitive embers is stated to occur in this shower. The text gives no count of how many genuine slow firebrands were removed and no count of how many spurious trajectories the filter removed.
- Broken-trajectory recombination (fragmentation handling) (Sec. 2.3.3 pp. 17 to 18, Fig. 9 caption p. 18, Table 3 p. 19): Within a specified volume (its bounds are not reported), each early-ended (EE) trajectory gets a list of late-started (LS) candidates whose start falls in a user-specified temporal search window that directly follows the EE end and has limited duration (6 timesteps 'in the present case'). A linear best fit (ref. [55]) on the EE data points, shown in the Fig. 9 caption as the last four EE points, predicts X, Y, Z versus time across the window. The first and second LS points are compared with predictions; per the Fig. 9 caption, 'in the present case' the tolerance is plus or minus 50 mm for the first point and c times tolerance = 75 mm for the second (relaxation factor c = 1.5), so the widening is from the first to the second LS point, not a function of the gap length. EE and LS are matched if both points fall within tolerance in all directions. Motivation: otherwise each portion is attributed to a new particle, so in size distribution analyses one particle could be counted several times and sizing data from sister trajectories would not be merged. The recombination is stated to be applied for the sizing analysis of Sec. 4.2.3; the text does not say whether it was applied to the trajectories used for the CPC/PNF flux counts or to Fig. 21a. The text does not explicitly confirm that the Fig. 9 tolerance values were the exact settings of the Table 3 run, although that is the natural reading.
- 3D shape reconstruction (visual hull) and resolution calibration (Sec. 2.4 pp. 19 to 22, Figs. 10 to 12; Sec. 3.2 pp. 25 to 26; Sec. 4.2.3 p. 33): Per timestep, silhouettes are segmented in each of the four views (Otsu), placed at the 3D particle location, rescaled with a per-camera resolution map (mm/pixel versus distance D to the camera reference plane, from a pole carrying six 25.4 mm white spheres moved to 22 locations), wrapped in convex envelopes and projected as cones; the intersection of the four cones is the hull, sampled by random points. Hulls from all timesteps (typically about 10 models) are reoriented by PCA and their orthogonal outlines accumulated for sizing. Known nonsystematic artifact: blind volumes not carved by the silhouette projection cones can remain and artificially enlarge the hull (seen along the y axis in Sec. 3.2 and the z axis in Sec. 4.2.3).
- Controlled tracking validation (Sec. 3.1, pp. 23 to 24, Figs. 13 and 14): Rotating lit punk stick (53.5 mm exposed length, about 3.6 mm thick) on a metallic arm (397 mm axis-to-axis) driven by a DC gear motor; the glowing lit end serves as a model firebrand. Recorded at 120 Hz over two complete revolutions, downsampled to 12 Hz (136 data points), compared with the trajectory predicted from the known layout (prediction uncertainty likely about plus or minus 5 mm in all directions). This is a single known glowing object; it tests 3D position and velocity accuracy, not detection precision, false positives or fragmentation.
- Exact controlled case behind the trajectory statistics (Sec. 4.1, pp. 27 to 28, Figs. 18 and 19): Artificial firebrand shower at the NIST National Fire Research Laboratory (NFRL): emberometer placed downstream of a firebrand generator (centrifugal blower Cincinnati Fan PB-9 in line with 149 mm I.D. stainless steel duct), operated to give showers whose particle number densities are visually consistent with field observations. Fuel: 350 g plus or minus 1 g of Douglas fir cuboids (7.8 mm, 8.1 mm, 12.9 mm, plus or minus 0.4 mm), moisture below 6 %. Blower at 5 Hz with two propane torches for 45 s, then propane stopped and blower at 20 Hz; a large flame settled at the outlet and disappeared after about 48 s; first firebrands about 16 s after flame onset, generation for about 74 s. Analysis restricted to a 35 s window starting 38 s after flame onset (peak production). Most landing firebrands were collected in water pans (about 2.4 m2, starting 0.56 m downstream of the generator exit) for an independent size dataset (999 firebrands). Ambient lighting and background conditions are not described, and the text does not explicitly say whether the test was indoors (it is contrasted with an outdoor fire).
- Flux metrics (Sec. 4.2.1, pp. 29 to 31, Figs. 21 to 23): Cumulative Particle Count (CPC, particles per m2) of trajectories crossing a reference disk (center X = -150 mm, Y = 1000 mm, Z = 0 mm, radius 200 mm); Particle Number Flux (PNF, particles per m2 per s) derived from CPC over a 30-timestep window (about 1 s). 'Firebrand rose' repeats this for reference-surface orientations in 15 degree increments (polar angle theta, azimuth phi).

The authors state that, based on the mean of the x, y, z sizes, 'an exact sphere size classification can be achieved' (Fig. 17 histogram comparison; no per-particle classification accuracy figure is given). Resolution about 1.6 mm per pixel at the back of the control volume.

- Camera relocation study (fictitious 25.4 mm sphere, computed reconstruction): with camera 4 moved out of plane, most data points fall below a 15 % volume overestimation level, a marked improvement over the original position.

Must not be transferred to wfglink:

- The 0.15 % (1 of 691) spurious-trajectory figure must not be quoted as a false-positive rate, precision, or expected spurious rate for wfglink. It comes from one laboratory firebrand-generator test at NIST NFRL with four calibrated, synchronized cameras, where (by the method described, my inference) a spurious detection also had to find epipolar correspondences in other views to become a 3D trajectory. The text gives no labeling protocol for deciding what was spurious, and the evidence indicates the count is a residual after a mean-displacement filter whose threshold and removal count are not reported.
- The 90 % recombination figure (87 of 97) and its parameters (6-timestep window, linear fit on the last 4 points, plus or minus 50 mm and 75 mm tolerances, relaxation 1.5) are defined in metric 3D object space at their unstated effective processing rate (about 30 timesteps per second by inference from Fig. 22). They must not be copied as pixel tolerances or frame counts, or presented as an expected wfglink fragmentation-repair rate.
- No numeric threshold for the mean-displacement filter exists in the text, so none may be cited. The text does not state the space or units of the operator; since it is applied to reconstructed 3D trajectories it likely uses 3D displacement (inference). In any case a single camera cannot tell a slow near ember from a fast far one or from a static bright spot at an unknown depth, so the same rule is weaker in pixel space.
- The text does not show that displacement filtering is safe for genuine embers. The evidence points the other way: it removed a trajectory the authors present as a real slow firebrand (Fig. 21b), and Sec. 2.3.3 notes that setting the operator to keep very slow firebrands can let spurious trajectories remain. Do not claim the filter has negligible recall cost; no count of removed genuine firebrands is given.
- Velocity values (below 0.4 m/s to above 4 m/s, 44.3 cm/s), position uncertainty (plus or minus 1.5 mm), speed uncertainty (plus or minus 1 %), and CPC/PNF uncertainty (minus 0, plus 4 %) all depend on multi-camera calibration and triangulation and do not transfer to a single uncalibrated RGB camera.
- Flux metrics (CPC 1281 or 1329 particles per m2, PNF 111.3 peak, firebrand rose, the 3x comparison with a prescribed fire) are specific to that shower, reference surface, and 3D geometry. wfglink counts are image-plane track counts and must not be expressed or compared in particles per m2 or per m2 per s.
- Do not cite 691 trajectories or 'nearly 700 firebrands' as a distinct-ember count or as a counting-accuracy benchmark; it includes a spurious trajectory and broken pieces, and the text does not say whether recombination was applied before the flux counts.
- Size results, visual hull accuracy, and resolution maps require four views and a resolution calibration in mm. A single camera cannot give metric ember size; do not cite their sizing accuracy for wfglink blob sizes.
- The robustness argument (Particle #145 recovered from other views when one view lost it) is a multi-view property. A single camera has no redundant view, so missing detections in wfglink become fragmentation or misses.
- The tracking-efficiency check uses a 3D test volume inside a 3D control volume. Its qualitative 'good tracking efficiency' conclusion is about their system and test only, not a benchmark for wfglink.
- The punk-stick validation tracks one known glowing object; it says nothing about detection precision, false positives, or fragmentation and must not be cited as evidence for any of those.
- Do not describe the emberometer results as field-validated wildfire data. The tracking statistics come from a controlled NFRL lab shower; field deployability is a design goal and outdoor emberometer results are not reported in this note (the outdoor comparison uses another study's ground-collection data). Ambient lighting, background, and whether the test was indoors are not described, so do not claim a dark, clean, or indoor scene either way.
- Their pipeline converts frames to 8-bit grayscale for detection and tracking. The paper provides no evidence about color cues for ember verification; do not cite it as support for any RGB color rule.
- The listed sources of spurious tracks (landed firebrand, bright background element) are examples only; no per-source statistics are given.
- Laser-dot synchronization and laser-beam orientation checks are multi-camera procedures; their sub-frame synchronization result has no meaning for a single camera.

Proposed adaptations (ours, not the authors' claims):

- Proposed adaptation: use the paper's track taxonomy (spurious, incomplete, broken, single) as audit labels for wfglink tracks on its own labeled clips, and report a Table 3 style breakdown with counts, a written labeling protocol, and an explicit statement of whether it is measured before or after verifier filtering, plus how many tracks each filter removed.
- Proposed adaptation: add a low-displacement rule to the rules stage (net or mean displacement over the first N frames), but treat it as a feature or soft score for the temporal classifier rather than a hard reject, because the paper shows such a filter removed a slow firebrand trajectory. Report the recall cost on labeled slow embers alongside the precision gain. Any threshold must be tuned on wfglink data in pixels and could be normalized by blob size since depth is unknown.
- Proposed adaptation: since the paper names landed firebrands and bright background elements as typical sources of spurious tracks, add a static bright-spot memory (per-location accumulator of long-lived, near-stationary detections) so candidates that sit on known static bright locations are rejected or down-weighted; this is a single-camera substitute for the cross-view check they had.
- Proposed adaptation: broken-track recombination before counting so one ember is not counted twice. Link an early-ended track to a late-started track within a short window using linear extrapolation of the last few image positions. Their method uses a fixed tolerance for the first matched point and a 1.5x wider tolerance for the second; a tolerance that grows with gap length would be a wfglink design choice, not something the paper did. To stay causal, delay the count of an ended track by the window length or merge before a count is committed. Window and pixel tolerances must be tuned on wfglink data.
- Proposed adaptation: per-frame tracked, new and lost counts inside an inner image region away from frame borders, used as a health monitor for tracker fragmentation and flicker; spikes in new or lost tracks without matching entry or exit events flag problems.
- Proposed adaptation: count only tracks that are accepted inside an inner counting region away from image borders, by analogy with their test volume kept well inside the control volume for efficiency monitoring (their own counts use crossings of a reference disk in the core flow), so border entry and exit are not confused with track starts and ends.
- Proposed adaptation: use an intensity-weighted centroid for sub-pixel blob positions, and Otsu thresholding inside a candidate patch to get a silhouette for simple shape features fed to the small patch classifier (in the paper Otsu is used only for sizing silhouettes, not detection).
- Proposed adaptation: replace the missing cross-view evidence with weaker temporal evidence: residual of a short linear or quadratic fit of the track, direction consistency, and smooth speed. Document clearly that this cannot recover depth, metric speed or metric size.
- Proposed adaptation: build a controlled positive test like their rotating punk stick (a lit incense or punk stick on a motor arm with known period) filmed by the single camera, to check that the tracker gives one unbroken track per revolution, measure fragmentation, and confirm slow-moving genuine glows are not rejected by the displacement rule (the paper itself used this rig only for position and velocity accuracy).
- Proposed adaptation: analog of their laser orientation check: compare against a reference frame or estimate global motion at the start of each clip and during runs, flagging camera bumps that make static bright background spots appear to move and could create false tracks.

Relevant methods:

- Multi-camera acquisition and synchronization (Sect. 2.1; Fig. 1; Fig. 2; Fig. 8 caption): Four compact cameras (Sony DSC-RX10 M3, about 20 MP; Sect. 3 calls the rig consumer-grade electronics) at the ends of two horizontal rails, at minimum focal length (8.8 mm) and largest aperture (f/2.4). Control volume, defined as the intersection of the camera fields of view, is about 1.91 m3. Streams were synchronized after acquisition using the disappearance of a laser dot visible to all cameras. Frames are converted to grayscale for tracking, with optional downsampling to cut computation for the 3D tracking step. A data acquisition frequency of 120 fps is quoted only for the expected velocity limits (Sect. 2.1). The effective tracking frame rate used in the NFRL test is not stated. The Fig. 8 caption equates a 30 timestep window with about 1 s, which implies about 30 timesteps per second, and the text does not reconcile this with 120 fps.
- Candidate detection (particle image localization) (Sect. 2.2, step (2)): OpenPTV finds firebrand 2D image coordinates with a pixel intensity-weighted centroid operator, which the paper says was 'confirmed to efficiently process glowing/hot particles'. That efficiency is not quantified.
- Multi-view correspondence and 3D triangulation (Sect. 2.2, steps (1), (3), (4), (5); Fig. 3): Particle correspondences across the four views use the epipolar line intersection technique, then 3D coordinates are computed in object space. Tracking is spatio-temporal particle matching using both image-space and object-space information (details deferred to Maas 1991; Maas et al. 1993; Willneff 2003). Calibration uses a custom 3D V-shaped target with 76 white dots. The paper does not describe any separate spurious-detection or spurious-trajectory rejection step, and it does not discuss whether or how multi-view correspondence suppresses distractors. Any view that it acts as an implicit consistency filter is reviewer inference, not a statement in the paper.
- Broken-trajectory recombination (fragmentation handling) (Sect. 2.2, second paragraph; Sect. 4.1): The paper says that, 'although seldom', a broken trajectory 'can occur if' a firebrand's visibility becomes challenging, and gives one example (low glow against a very bright background object). It does not give a full list of causes. Dedicated algorithms predict, within a user-specified temporal window, the missing positions of firebrands whose trajectories ended early, then identify 'sister' trajectories by spatio-temporal matching in each particle image plane. The window length, prediction model and matching thresholds are not given in the main text; the paper defers to Bouvet et al. 2020 (NIST TN 2093). The stated purpose in Sect. 4.1 is 'to support the airborne firebrand sizing analysis', with avoiding counting broken tracks as independent particles given in parentheses as a second aim.
- Operating density (Sect. 2.2, third paragraph): Particle image densities on the order of 4 x 10^-5 particles per pixel are 'considered' in this work. The paper says this is similar to density estimates from field work data (e.g. Filkov and Prohanov 2018), one to two orders of magnitude below large-scale PTV with controlled seeding, and two orders below densities known to promote triangulation errors (about 5 x 10^-3 ppp, Maas et al. 1993).
- Resolvability limits derived from imaging geometry (Sect. 2.1, second paragraph): Minimum particle size of 2 mm is a conservative estimate from the lowest spatial resolution in the control volume and a minimum particle image size of 1 pixel. The lower velocity limit (0.27 m/s) assumes a 1-pixel particle image diameter (no subpixel accuracy), a 1-pixel diagonal displacement, 1.6 mm/pixel, visibility in only two consecutive frames, and 120 fps. The paper notes the limit can be lower when a particle is visible in more frames, because intermediate frames can be skipped.
- Per-particle crop background removal and segmentation (sizing stage only) (Sect. 2.3, step 1 and the following paragraph): Using 2D coordinates from the 3D-PTV output, each image is cropped and the median image of the prior 10 images is subtracted 'to improve particle isolation and remove background objects'. The boundary is then found with Otsu unsupervised threshold selection. The Otsu method 'was visually confirmed to perform very well overall in the current configurations'. The paper notes a possible failure mode, not a counted result: instances where a portion of the particle blends with the background 'may negatively influence edge detection in individual images'.
- Multi-observation aggregation per track (sizing) (Sect. 2.3, step 3 and the following paragraph; Fig. 4): All available 3D reconstructions of a particle, typically about 10 models, are re-oriented with PCA along their main axis, and their projected outlines are pooled into cumulative outline plots. The paper says aggregating multiple observations of the same particle 'helps minimize the weight of those sporadic cases' (background blending, hidden features) in the final sizing results.
- Validation design: known-trajectory glowing target (Sect. 3.1): A rotating punk stick (about 3.6 mm thick) on a metallic arm about 397 mm long, mounted on the shaft of a gear box motor controlled by a DC power supply. Its glowing lit end is a model firebrand. Measured radius, height and velocity are compared with values predicted from the setup geometry. This tests geometric tracking accuracy, not detection precision against distractors.
- Validation design: known-size released particles (Sect. 3 introduction; Sect. 3.2; Fig. 5): 110 quasi-spherical particles in 5 size classes (6, 8, 10, 12, 14 mm) released randomly in space and time from a 50.8 cm x 76.2 cm platform with 19 equally distributed 16 mm holes, about 1.65 m above the optical table, lit by four 3200-lumen LED lights. Sect. 3.2 does not state the sphere material. The Sect. 3 introduction says the validation tests used combusting model embers and non-combusting white particles, so the LED-lit spheres were presumably the non-combusting ones. Measured size histograms are compared with known diameters. Size classes were deliberately close together to make the test stringent.
- Validation design: real combusting firebrand shower with an independent reference (Sect. 4.1; Sect. 4.2.3; Fig. 6; Fig. 10): A firebrand generator at the NIST National Fire Research Laboratory, fed 350 g of dry (moisture content < 6%) Douglas fir cuboids, produced firebrands for about 74 s. The Sect. 4.2 analysis covers a 35 s peak production window. Water pans covering about 2.4 m2 collected most of the landing firebrands (999 in all), which were dried and measured in 2D as an independent size reference. The comparison is distribution against distribution, not per-particle matching. The lighting conditions for this test are not described.
- Count metric from particle crossings and its sensitivity check (Sect. 4.2.1; Figs 7, 8, 9): Cumulative Particle Count (CPC, unit particles m-2) is defined from 'the number of particles crossing a reference surface of defined geometry and location', expressed per unit area of that surface. The reference surface is a disk (center X = -150 mm, Y = 1000 mm, Z = 0 mm; radius 200 mm) placed in the core of the flow at mid-height of the control volume. Particle Number Flux (PNF, particles m-2 s-1) is derived from the time-resolved CPC, with derivations over a 30 timestep window, stated as about 1 s in the Fig. 8 caption. Sensitivity was tested by changing the disk area by plus or minus 15%. A 'firebrand rose' repeats CPC and PNF over reference-surface orientations in 15 degree increments, keeping the surface center fixed.
- Synthetic walk-through for reconstruction error (Sect. 5; Fig. 11): A fictitious 25.4 mm sphere is moved through the control volume and its visual hull reconstructed for two positions of Camera 4. Normalized volume (reconstructed over known volume) is plotted against the mean camera-to-particle distance. As future work, the paper proposes a hybrid approach with a deep learning shape classifier, supported by synthetic data generation, to correct sizing bias.

Must not be transferred to wfglink:

- The 6.4% recombined and 0.8% unrecoverable fragmentation rates. They come from a calibrated 4-camera 3D-PTV system (acquisition quoted at 120 fps in Sect. 2.1; effective tracking rate for this test not stated), a laboratory firebrand generator at NIST NFRL, Douglas fir cuboids and very low image density (about 4 x 10^-5 ppp). wfglink is single-camera RGB without depth, often compressed video, with uncontrolled backgrounds. These rates must not be quoted as expected or achievable wfglink fragmentation rates.
- Do not treat the fragmentation statistic as precision or as a spurious-track rate. The paper reports no precision, false-positive or spurious-trajectory figure, so it cannot be cited as evidence for any precision level of an ember track verifier.
- Reviewer inference, not a claim in the paper: the 4-view epipolar correspondence and triangulation probably keep many single-view artifacts from becoming 3D trajectories. The paper never discusses clutter, distractors or their rejection, so it must not be cited as showing that its trajectory set was clean. wfglink has no second view, so it would have no such filtering. Even if the multi-view trajectory set was clean, that would not imply a clean single-camera candidate set.
- The claim that broken trajectories are 'seldom' holds, if at all, under the paper's conditions (low density, four views, tracking in image and object space). It must not be assumed for wfglink, where motion blur, compression, exposure changes and bright backgrounds are likely to fragment tracks more often.
- Metric quantities (2 mm minimum size, 0.27 to 18 m/s velocity range, 1.6 mm/pixel, CPC and PNF in particles per m2 and per m2 per s, the firebrand rose) all need calibrated 3D positions and metric scale. wfglink counts are image-plane track counts, not fluxes per area, and must not be expressed in these units or compared with these values.
- The recombination algorithm's parameters (temporal window, prediction model, matching thresholds) are not in this paper; they are deferred to NIST TN 2093, which was not read here. Do not attribute specific parameter values to this source.
- 'Confirmed to efficiently process glowing/hot particles' (intensity-weighted centroid) and Otsu thresholding 'visually confirmed to perform very well' are unquantified qualitative statements made under the paper's configurations. They are not evidence of detector performance on wildfire video.
- The median of prior 10 frames background subtraction was applied only to crops around particles already located by 3D-PTV, for sizing, with fixed-position cameras. It was not a detection or verification step, and it was not validated against distractors.
- The validation experiments test geometric tracking and sizing accuracy on known targets: one glowing stick end, and LED-lit quasi-spherical particles of known size (presumably the non-combusting white particles mentioned in Sect. 3). None measures rejection of non-ember lookalikes. They must not be presented as precision validation.
- All Visual Hull sizing results, size histograms and the 15% volume overestimation figure depend on multi-view reconstruction. They have no meaning for single-camera wfglink.
- The comparison with Thomas et al. 2017 (artificial flux more than 3 times the authors' estimated maximum for that one prescribed fire) is about how realistic the generator's exposure is. It says nothing about detection and must not be used to argue about ember density in wfglink clips.
- The internal ambiguity between 691 raw trajectories, 640 expected complete trajectories and 'nearly 700 firebrands' is not resolved in the text. Do not derive a fragment-per-particle ratio or an overcount rate from these numbers.

Proposed adaptations (ours, not the authors' claims):

- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: add image-plane gap bridging to the wfglink tracker, modeled on the paper's recombination idea. When a track ends early, predict its missing positions forward for a short user-set window of frames. Then link it to a 'sister' track that starts near the predicted position at a consistent time. The merged track counts once. In the causal verifier, the window must close before the accept or reject decision, so the bridging window bounds verifier latency. Window, motion model and thresholds must be chosen and validated on wfglink data, since the paper gives none.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: report fragmentation separately from precision, using the paper's three-way breakdown. On human-labeled wfglink clips, count expected complete ember tracks, the fraction that fragmented but were correctly re-linked, and the fraction that stayed fragmented. Also report spurious accepted tracks, a quantity the paper does not provide. This keeps fragmentation overcounting from being confused with false positives.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: build a known-trajectory true-positive check like the rotating punk stick. Film a glowing stick end on a motor-driven arm with known radius and angular speed using the single wfglink camera. Check that the tracker yields one continuous track per revolution segment, that the verifier accepts it, and that the fitted image-plane circle matches the geometry. This tests continuity and acceptance, not rejection of lookalikes. Any such rig must be run with appropriate fire safety supervision.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: add the condition the paper gives as an example cause of broken trajectories, a low-glow ember against a very bright background object, to the wfglink test sets for both the tracker and the verifier, as a hard true-positive case.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: in the small patch classifier input, add a causal background-subtracted channel. Subtract the median of the prior N frames from the crop around the candidate. The paper used N = 10, but only for sizing crops taken with fixed cameras. Validate on wfglink clips, because a moving camera, flicker or compression could make this channel misleading.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: decide per track rather than per frame. Pool patch classifier scores over the available observations of a track within the causal window. This borrows the paper's sizing rationale that aggregating multiple observations reduces the weight of sporadic bad frames. Set a minimum observation count before acceptance. The paper applied this to sizing, not to classification, so the benefit must be shown on wfglink data.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: derive per-clip resolvability limits the way the paper does (1-pixel minimum image size, 1-pixel minimum displacement, two-frame visibility), but in pixel and frame units. Use them to set minimum track length and minimum displacement rules. The paper resolved slow embers (< 0.4 m/s), so a strict minimum-motion rule may reject real slow embers. Check any such rule against labeled slow embers before adopting it.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: run a sensitivity check on counting-region parameters, as the paper varied disk area by plus or minus 15%. Report how wfglink accepted-track counts change when the counting region or line and the verifier thresholds are perturbed.
- PROPOSED ADAPTATION: when possible, compare wfglink counts with an independent reference of a different kind, such as an independent human count of the same clip window, as the paper compared airborne measurements with ground collection. Compare like populations and the same time window.

Checker: 11 problems found and corrected; access level judged correct.

Relevant methods:

- Task definition: two-class combustion-state classifier (Section 3, pp.4 to 5; Figure 2, p.5): The CNN classifies each view of an already detected and 3D-tracked airborne firebrand as 'flaming' or 'smoldering'. Every input is a known firebrand. The paper has no background, clutter or non-firebrand class and no detection step inside the CNN. Each quadrant 'may be independently rated' flaming or smoldering, and a firebrand can be flaming in some views and smoldering in others at the same time step (the 'Mixed' column of Fig. 2). So the unit that is labeled and classified is a single image (one view at one time step). The paper does not state whether a particle's label can change from one time step to the next, and it also describes the 293 particles as 'hand labeled as smoldering or flaming'.
- Upstream detection and tracking (not part of the CNN) (Section 2, pp.3 to 4; Section 3, p.4; Figure 2 caption, p.5): The Emberometer combines 3D Particle Tracking Velocimetry and 3D Particle Shape Reconstruction with four cameras. Video frames are extracted as high bit depth color sequences with commercial software. An in-house MATLAB code synchronizes them and converts them to low bit depth grayscale. The grayscale sequences are 'processed by the PTV software' (the paper does not say whether it is commercial or in-house), and in-house FORTRAN routines assemble each particle's full 3D motion history and patch broken trajectories. The classifier uses high bit depth color crops (background subtracted), cropped and numbered from the final tracking outputs. A quadrant is entirely black when the firebrand was not detected in that view at that time step (not visible, or detected but the particle correspondence step failed). Black quadrants are identified automatically and removed from CNN analysis.
- Input crops (Section 3, p.4; Section 2, p.4; Figure 2, p.5): Each camera view of a firebrand at one time step goes to the CNN on its own as an RGB image of 71 x 71 pixels, for both training and routine classification. Up to four views make up a 'quadview', but the views are not fused. CNN inputs are background subtracted (Section 2 text and Fig. 2 caption). The paper does not say how the 71 x 71 crop is placed or scaled relative to particle size, what the background model is, or whether the high bit depth was kept at the CNN input. It also does not say how the 71 x 71 crops were resized to the input sizes the pretrained networks expect.
- Imaging system (Section 2, p.3; Figure 1, p.3): Four Sony DSC-RX10M3 compact cameras (about 20 megapixels) with large diameter built-in lenses. The cameras were given full spectrum conversion and fitted with Hoya R72 infrared filters 'ensuring light transmission above 760 nm', 'to enhance firebrand detection'. They sit in hard shells with quartz windows on a collapsible X-shaped aluminum stand. Video is recorded and frames are extracted with commercial software. The paper states no frame rate, exposure or resolution of the extracted frames. The example frame in Fig. 1b looks magenta-toned (checker and extractor visual observation, not a statement in the text).
- Experimental scene and fuel (Section 2, p.4): Outdoor deployment at the Frederick County Public Safety Training Facility, Frederick, MD, downstream of a firebrand generator (a centrifugal blower in line with cylindrical stainless-steel duct elements). Fuel was 350 g plus or minus 1 g of dry (moisture under 6 %) birch/maple dowels, 6.4 mm diameter and 51 mm long, with chamfered edges. The background was asphalt, forested areas with mixed vegetation, and clear sky. The text does not state the time of day.
- Hand labeling protocol (Section 3, p.5): 293 particles were 'carefully reviewed and hand labeled as smoldering or flaming'. The review covered all time steps and quadrant views available for each firebrand, excluding black quadrants and quadrants where large flames attributed to the firebrand generator were visible. Labeling used color images without background subtraction, 'allowing for detailed dynamic examination of the firebrand silhouettes across time steps'. So labelers had temporal context that the single-frame CNN does not have (extractor inference). This produced 23,873 labeled images. The paper does not say how many people labeled and reports no inter-labeler agreement.
- Class balancing and data split (Section 3, pp.5 to 6; Section 4, p.8): 88.8 % of the labeled images were smoldering. To avoid bias, the authors drew a random subset of smoldering images equal in size to the flaming set (about 2,700 each). The balanced set was split randomly into training (80 %), validation (10 %) and test (10 %) and held constant across all CNNs. The paper describes no grouping by particle, time step or view, so images of the same firebrand (other time steps or other views) can appear in more than one split (extractor inference from 'separated randomly'). About 18,500 leftover smoldering images, 'previously set apart during the training dataset generation step', were later classified with the chosen model.
- Lightweight from-scratch CNN (A to D) (Section 3, p.4; Section 4, p.7; Figure 3, p.6; Table 1, p.7): Seven layers in Fig. 3: L1 conv + batch norm + ReLU, L2 max pool 2x2 stride 2, L3 conv + BN + ReLU, L4 max pool 2x2 stride 2, L5 conv + BN + ReLU, L6 fully connected (2 outputs), L7 softmax. Feature maps run 71x71, then 35x35, then 17x17. The filter configurations (Table 1) are A: 3x3 with 28, 22, 16 filters; B: 3x3 with 64, 128, 256; C: 5x5 with 28, 22, 16; D: 7x7 with 28, 22, 16. Parametric tests varied filter size and filter count. Adding convolutional layers had little effect. Table 1 shows only 'A representative subset' of the configurations; other filter combinations were trained but 'the performance metrics were lower than those presented'. The paper does not say whether the shown configurations were picked on validation or test results.
- Transfer learning on five ImageNet-pretrained networks (E to I) (Section 3, pp.4 to 5; NIST landing page abstract): The PDF describes them by structure only. E: 5 conv and 3 FC layers, 61 million parameters, overlapping pooling and local response normalization. F: 22 layers, 7 million parameters, 9 inception modules. G: bottleneck design, 18 layers, 11.7 million parameters, residual mapping, skip connections, heavy batch norm. H: 18 layers, 1.24 million parameters, 8 fire modules. I: 16 layers, 138 million parameters, all 3x3 convolutions. They were 'chosen due to availability, prevalence in the literature, and variation in architecture'. The NIST landing page abstract names the set as AlexNet, GoogLeNet, ResNet-18, SqueezeNet and VGG-16 and says the chosen one is GoogLeNet; since the PDF says the chosen one is CNN F, F is GoogLeNet. Matching E, G, H and I one by one to the other names is extractor inference from the structure descriptions; the PDF does not state it.
- Training settings (Section 3, p.6; Section 4, pp.7 to 8): MATLAB Deep Learning Toolbox (MATLAB 2022, reference [19] gives R2022a). Learning rate 0.0001, chosen from validation loss trends. The number of iterations was set by a validation patience of 5. For the transfer learning cases only, 'the learning rate for the weights and biases was increased by a factor of ten' to shorten training (the paper does not say which layers this applied to), and the mini-batch size was varied between 5 and 15: 5 gave the Table 1 results and 10 and 15 were 'marginally worse for every pretrained CNN'. The mini-batch size for lightweight CNNs A to D is not stated. No optimizer is named. Hardware: Intel i7-8700 with NVIDIA Quadro P1000 (4 GB). CNN I used a Xeon W-1370P with Quadro RTX 4000 (8 GB). The paper reports no data augmentation and no inference speed.
- Metrics and model selection (Section 3.1, pp.6 to 7; Section 4, pp.7 to 8; Section 5, p.8): Accuracy (Eq. 1) and per-class precision, recall and F-measure (Eqs. 2 and 3). Validation and test accuracy were compared to check for overfitting, and per-class F-measures to check bias. The final choice weighed the performance metrics and 'memory requirements': those of E and I were about 10 and 22 times that of F, and I needed additional computing resources. These ratios match the Table 1 size-on-disk column (202/21.2 is about 9.5 and 477/21.2 is about 22.5, checker arithmetic). The conclusions describe the basis as 'accuracy, F-measure, and size metrics'. CNN F was chosen. The paper gives no confidence intervals and no repeated runs or seeds.

- Dataset size: 293 hand-labeled particles gave 23,873 single-view, single-time-step images labeled flaming or smoldering, after excluding black quadrants and quadrants showing large generator flames. 88.8 % were smoldering. Source: one outdoor Emberometer test with birch/maple dowels from a blower-driven firebrand generator. They criticize Govil et al. (2020) for a test set of 250 hand-selected images that contained images from the same video sequences as training, with only 10 min (10 frames) of separation, which may overstate performance.

Must not be transferred to wfglink:

- Smoke as proof of embers. FIgLib labels are whether smoke is visible in a remote mountaintop fixed-camera scene, at about 1 frame per minute. The paper neither labels nor discusses embers (its only finer annotations are smoke boxes and masks for the 144 train fires). Nothing in this paper links smoke presence to ember presence, count or position, so smoke must not count as evidence that a candidate is an ember.
- A mandatory smoke gate. The paper gives no evidence that requiring smoke improves ember precision. Design reasoning, not from the source: visible embers can occur without visible smoke in frame, and smoke occurs without embers, so a gate would add a second error source with unknown recall and could silently drop true ember tracks. Smoke may at most be an optional context feature that has to win an ablation.
- Any SmokeyNet number as a target or baseline for wfglink: 83.49% accuracy, 89.84% precision, 76.45% recall, 82.59% F1, 3.12 min TTD. These are image-level smoke classifications on a FIgLib test split drawn from a roughly balanced dataset. They are not track-level, not ember-level and not count-level, and do not reflect real-world prevalence.
- Precision values in general. The paper itself warns that the 50/50 balance is not representative of real-world scenarios and may induce a higher false positive rate on real-world data. Precision measured on a balanced set says nothing about precision at wfglink's real candidate prevalence.
- The claim that FIgLib's splitting by fires ensures 'no data related to the test set are in the training set'. The published lists show the split unit is a camera sequence. The same named event appears in train and test from different cameras, most test cameras and stations also appear in train, and validation and test share 5 named fires. The split is neither camera-disjoint nor site-disjoint, and not strictly event-disjoint. wfglink should not copy this grouping key as if it prevented leakage.
- The temporal design and its ablation conclusions. SmokeyNet's temporal context is 2 or 3 frames about 60 s apart, which captures plume growth. wfglink candidates are tracked at video frame rate over far shorter time scales (a wfglink-side fact, not from this source), so the paper's finding that a third frame only marginally improves performance, and the Table 2 pattern that the LSTM adds about 1 accuracy point over ResNet34 + ViT, do not carry over to ember trajectories.
- The Table 2 pattern that adding the ViT gave the largest precision gain in the ablation (my reading of the table; the paper says it 'significantly improves performance'). That concerns amorphous smoke spread across 45 large tiles. It has not been shown for small point-like ember candidates.
- Tuned constants: 224 px tiles with 20 px overlap, 45 tiles, a 250-pixel tile label threshold, positive weights 5 and 40, resize to 1392x1856, and a crop of the top 352 rows. These depend on FIgLib resolution, horizon geometry and class ratios.
- Positive-class upweighting as a default. The paper describes raising the image positive weight as trading precision for higher recall, and chose 5 for balanced precision and recall and better accuracy and F1. Recall-leaning upweighting runs against wfglink's precision-first goal.
- FIgLib's negative taxonomy (clouds, fog, haze, glare, smog) as a complete list of ember confusers. Ember false positives come from different sources (specular glints, artificial lights, non-wildfire sparks, insects, hot pixels, compression flicker, sunlit leaves; wfglink-side list, not from the source). Clouds and fog matter mostly as background.
- FIgLib images or labels as training data for the ember patch or temporal classifier. The frame rate is about 1 per minute, the scenes are wide mountaintop views, and the labels are smoke-only, so neither ember positives nor useful temporal negatives for ember tracks exist there.
- The human-parity claim ('rivals human performance'). The human baseline used 62 images and 3 lab members, and the model's figures come from the full test set. It says nothing about human or model ember counting.
- The counterfactual 85.8% accuracy and 94.8% precision. It is hypothetical, not a measured result.
- The Govil et al. field-test figure (21% of notifications showed smoke from real fires after 1-hour suppression). It is a secondhand report on a different system, cameras and protocol, and is not a calibration target.
- Runtime and model size (56.9M params, 51.6 ms/image, measurement hardware not stated). These do not represent wfglink's target hardware or a small patch classifier.

Proposed adaptations (ours, not the authors' claims):

- Proposed adaptation: group splits by physical event and recording session, not by clip file or camera view. Keep every clip and view of the same burn or recording in a single split. Store the split lists as versioned files, as FIgLib published its lists. Normalize names before checking (FIgLib's own list has one malformed name that hides a shared key). Run an automatic overlap check (event, session, camera, location, date) that fails the build if keys are shared between splits. Report any camera or site overlap that remains, rather than claiming the split is leakage-free.
- Proposed adaptation: seal the test set and keep validation and test event-disjoint too. Use validation only for early stopping, threshold choice and calibration. FIgLib's validation and test share named fires, and FIgLib selects the model by lowest validation loss, so information can leak through model selection.
- Proposed adaptation: if hard negatives are mined from held-out data, split the held-out pool and leave one part untouched as the sealed test. This follows the procedure the authors propose (but did not carry out) in Section 7.4.
- Proposed adaptation: make accepted-track precision the primary metric, defined exactly (an accepted track counts as TP if matched to a labeled ember track under a stated matching rule). Report recall, F1 and count error next to it. Always state the positive prevalence of the evaluation set. Also report false accepts per minute of ember-free video, because balanced evaluation inflates precision.
- Proposed adaptation: define a causal time-to-accept for each true ember track, in frames and ms from track birth to verifier acceptance, with the time origin stated. Report never-accepted tracks separately as misses instead of leaving their handling unstated, as FIgLib's TTD does. Always pair it with false accepts per minute, because the FIgLib authors attribute ResNet50's fastest TTD to an unacceptable amount of false positives.
- Proposed adaptation: make labels offline with full past and future context, but evaluate the verifier strictly causally. Report early-track performance (the first N frames after birth) separately, since FIgLib reports that early, faint smoke is hard under limited causal context (human baseline) and is a common miss (Figure 3 caption).
- Proposed adaptation: build an ablation table modeled on Table 2. Rows: rules only; single-patch classifier; patch plus short causal frame stack; patch plus track-feature sequence; optional background-subtraction channel; optional context window around the patch. Use the same grouped splits for every row, average several runs (FIgLib averaged five), report the spread across runs (FIgLib did not), and report params and ms per candidate with the hardware stated.
- Proposed adaptation: test a background-subtraction or frame-difference channel as an optional input to the patch classifier. In FIgLib, MOG2 mainly raised recall at higher size and time cost. Keep it only if it raises precision or recall at a fixed precision on validation.
- Proposed adaptation: do not raise frame-level or clip-level alarms by OR over candidates; decide per track and count only accepted tracks. FIgLib reports that the any-tile-positive rule did worse for tile-to-image aggregation in non-ViT baselines, but gives no numbers, so this is weak supporting evidence, not a measured result for tracks.
- Proposed adaptation: create a labeled hard-negative set with ember-specific categories (glints, lights, non-wildfire sparks, insects, hot pixels, compression flicker, bright foliage) and report the false accept rate per category. Include same-scene clips from before embers appear, analogous to FIgLib's 40 min of pre-ignition frames, so negatives share background with positives.
- Proposed adaptation: for precision-first operation, do not upweight positives by default. Choose the acceptance threshold on validation to meet a stated target precision, then report recall at that operating point on the sealed test.
- Proposed adaptation: add a per-clip timeline plot of accepted and rejected tracks over time, colored by correct or incorrect (modeled on Figure 3). Use it for error audits, and name systematic false positive and miss categories (for example occlusion, faint start, fade-out), as Section 6.2 does.
- Proposed adaptation: a static region-of-interest mask (for example sky, or known fixed bright sources) is allowed only if it is fixed per clip before any test results are seen, documented, and judged on validation. This is the analog of FIgLib's horizon crop, without its constants.
- Proposed adaptation: if smoke or haze cues are ever used, make them an optional non-gating feature that must show a validation gain in the ablation table. Never make a smoke check a required stage before counting.

Relevant methods:

- Problem framing: class imbalance and classic bootstrapping (hard negative mining) (Section 1 (p.1 to 2); Section 2, paragraph 'Hard example mining' (p.2)): Detection reduced to classification creates a large imbalance between object and background examples. The paper says this can be as extreme as 100,000 background examples to every one object for sliding-window detectors such as DPM, and may still be high (e.g. 70:1) for proposal-based detectors. It names two classic mining algorithms. (1) For SVMs: keep a working set, train to convergence, remove easy examples (correctly classified beyond the margin), add hard ones (margin violators); this leads to the global SVM solution. (2) For non-SVM models: train on positives plus random negatives, run the model on a larger set to harvest false positives, add them, and retrain; this is usually iterated once and has no convergence proof. Both follow an alternation template: a fixed model finds examples, then the model trains on a fixed active set. The paper argues that freezing a deep ConvNet trained with SGD over hundreds of thousands of steps, even for a few iterations at a time, would dramatically slow progress, so a purely online form is needed.
- Baseline heuristic minibatch sampling in Fast R-CNN (FRCN) (Section 3.1 'Training', paragraphs 'Foreground RoIs', 'Background RoIs', 'Balancing fg-bg RoIs' (p.3)): Each SGD minibatch samples N images and then B/N RoIs per image, with N=2 and B=128. The per-RoI loss is a classification log loss plus a localization loss. Foreground: IoU with ground truth of at least 0.5. Background: maximum IoU with ground truth in [bg_lo, 0.5), with bg_lo=0.1 (used by FRCN and SPPnet). The paper says this bg_lo rule 'is hypothesized in [14] to crudely approximate hard negative mining', on the assumption that partly overlapping regions are the confusing ones. Foreground to background is rebalanced to 1:3 (25% foreground) by randomly undersampling background. The authors state that removing this ratio (random sampling) or increasing it lowers FRCN accuracy by about 3 points mAP (no table given).
- Online hard example mining (OHEM): loss-based selection inside each minibatch (Section 1 (p.2); Sections 4.1 and 4.2 (p.4 to 5); Figure 2 (p.5)): At SGD iteration t, compute the conv feature map for each image. Run the RoI network forward on ALL input RoIs R (Figure 2 labels the selective search RoIs of an image as |R| about 2000; Section 4.2 says N=2 gives |R| about 4000), not on a sampled subset. Compute each RoI's loss (classification plus bbox regression) under the current network, sort the RoIs by loss, and keep the hardest. Section 4.1 phrases this per input image as 'taking the B/N examples'; Section 4.2 says all RoIs from all N images are used as R, and the Figure 2 caption says the module selects B examples (B=128). The paper does not spell out whether the top-K cut is applied per image or over the pooled R. Only the selected RoIs go through forward and backward passes. The model is in effect frozen for exactly one minibatch, so it updates as often as baseline SGD. The extra forward cost is 'relatively small' because computation is shared through the conv feature map. The backward pass is 'no more expensive than before', because only the selected RoIs are backpropagated. The Introduction describes the selection as sampling from a non-uniform, non-stationary distribution that depends on the current loss and favors diverse, high-loss instances.
- NMS deduplication among candidate RoIs, ranked by loss (Section 4.1, fourth paragraph (p.4); Figure 2 (p.5)): Co-located RoIs with high overlap are likely to have correlated losses. They can also project onto the same region of the conv feature map because of resolution disparity, which leads to loss double counting. Standard NMS (the FRCN implementation) is applied with loss as the score: repeatedly take the RoI with the highest loss, then remove lower-loss RoIs that overlap it heavily. A relaxed IoU threshold of 0.7 suppresses only highly overlapping RoIs. The Figure 2 diagram labels the selection module 'Diverse & Hard RoI Sampler'; the caption calls it the 'Hard RoI module'.
- Removal of the bg_lo threshold and the fg-bg ratio (Section 4.1, last paragraph (p.4); Section 3.1 (p.3)): OHEM uses bg_lo=0 (Table 1 rows 11 to 13) and no fixed foreground to background ratio. The argument: if a class were neglected, its loss would rise until it is likely to be sampled. A minibatch may be all background when foreground is easy (e.g. a canonical view of a car), or all foreground when background is trivial (sky, grass).
- Implementation: read-only RoI network versus loss masking (Section 4.2 (p.4 to 5); Figure 2 (p.5)): Option 1: compute loss for all RoIs in the loss layer, sort, and set non-hard losses to 0. This is straightforward but inefficient, because memory is still allocated and a backward pass is run for every RoI. Option 2 (used): keep two copies of the RoI network with shared weights (Figure 2 legend). The read-only copy runs forward only on all RoIs; the hard RoI sampling module selects R_hard-sel; the regular copy runs forward and backward only on R_hard-sel, accumulates the gradients and passes them to the conv network. Implemented in Caffe with gradient accumulation over N single-image forward-backward passes. With N=2, |R| about 4000 and B=128, the authors report option 2 has a similar memory footprint to option 1 but is more than 2 times faster.
- Analysis protocol (Section 5.1 (p.5)): Two backbones: VGG_CNN_M_1024 (VGGM, a wider version of AlexNet) and VGG16. Train on PASCAL VOC 2007 trainval and test on VOC 2007 test. SGD for 80k iterations, initial LR 0.001, decayed by 0.1 every 30k iterations. Baselines (Table 1 rows 1 and 2) were reproduced on this schedule and are slightly higher than in the original FRCN paper. The paper reports one number per configuration and says nothing about seeds, repeated runs or variance.
- Controlled comparisons: OHEM versus heuristic sampling versus using all RoIs (Sections 5.2, 5.3, 5.4 and Table 1 (p.5 to 6)): Conditions: FRCN with bg_lo=0.1 versus bg_lo=0. N=1 versus N=2 images per batch, run 'in order to increase correlation in an effort to break our method'. Standard FRCN with a large minibatch B=2048, bg_lo=0 and N in {1,2}, with LR retuned to 0.003 (VGG16) and 0.004 (VGGM), as the 'train with all the RoIs and let easy ones contribute little gradient' alternative. OHEM with B=128 and bg_lo=0. Results are in Table 1.
- Sampling-independent training loss measurement (Section 5.5 and Figure 3 (p.6)): To compare methods fairly regardless of how each samples examples, model snapshots taken every 20k steps are run over the entire VOC07 trainval set, and the average loss per RoI over all RoIs is reported for the VGG16 variants (Figure 3). Figure 3 has two panels: one for N=2 runs (FRCN, FRCN bg_lo=0, FRCN bg_lo=0 B=2048, Our Approach bg_lo=0) and one for the corresponding N=1 runs.
- Explanation of why OHEM beats heuristic sampling (as argued in the paper) (Section 3.1 (p.3); Section 4 opening and 4.1 (p.4); Sections 5.2 to 5.5 (p.5 to 6)): (a) The bg_lo heuristic 'helps convergence and detection accuracy' but is suboptimal because it ignores some infrequent but important difficult background regions. (b) The 1:3 ratio is a hyperparameter that the authors say OHEM can remove 'with no ill effect', because a neglected class's loss rises until it is sampled, so the mix of foreground and background in a minibatch can follow what is currently hard in each image (my paraphrase of Sections 3.1 and 4.1). (c) Selection happens every iteration with the current model, so 'learning is not delayed'. (d) Empirically, OHEM reaches the lowest full-set training loss (Figure 3) and the highest test mAP in Table 1, and beats the use-all-RoIs variant. The authors also say OHEM trains faster than the use-all-RoIs variant because it computes gradients with a smaller minibatch, but they report no timing for the B=2048 runs. The paper gives empirical support but no formal analysis of why use-all-RoIs underperforms.
- Computational cost measurement (Section 5.6 and Table 2 (p.6)): Seconds per training iteration and maximum memory on one Nvidia Titan X GPU, for FRCN and OHEM with VGGM and VGG16. Some VGG16 settings use gradient accumulation over two forward/backward passes.
- Benchmark evaluation and complementary additions (Sections 6 and 7, Tables 3 to 6 (p.6 to 8)): VGG16 on VOC07, VOC12 and MS COCO test-dev 2015. Schedules: VOC07 trainval uses the Section 5 schedule; 200k iterations with LR step 40k for the extra-data VOC sets (07+12 and 07++12); 240k iterations with step 160k for COCO. Additions: multi-scale training and testing (M; train s in {480,576,688,864,900}, test s in {480,576,688,864,1000}, max side capped at 1000, chosen because of GPU memory constraints) and iterative bbox regression with weighted voting (B; NMS at IoU 0.3, voting with boxes at IoU of at least 0.5). Table 6 ablates M (train and test separately) and B with and without OHEM.
- Concurrent loss-based selection works cited (not studied in this paper) (Section 2, paragraph 'Hard example selection in deep learning' (p.2)): [27] Simo-Serra et al. independently select hard positive and negative examples from a larger set of random examples based on their loss, to learn image descriptors. [33] Wang and Gupta, given a positive pair of patches, find hard negative patches from a large set using a triplet loss. [22] Loshchilov and Hutter investigate online loss-based selection of hard examples for minibatch SGD, focused on ConvNets for image classification. The paper only names these and gives no results for them.

Must not be transferred to wfglink:

- None of the mAP or AP gains (for example +2.7 mAP on VOC07 with VGG16, or +2.9 AP on COCO) may be cited as an expected improvement for wfglink. They come from 2016 Fast R-CNN with VGG backbones and selective search proposals on PASCAL VOC and MS COCO still images. The paper has no ember, fire, night, glow, video or single-camera RGB streaming data.
- mAP and AP summarize ranking quality over all detections, averaged over classes, and for COCO also over recall and IoU levels. They are not precision at a chosen threshold, false accepted tracks per minute, or count error. The paper does not show that OHEM raises precision at a high-confidence operating point, which is what a precision-first verifier needs.
- Every number is a single reported value with no seeds, repeats or variance. Differences of about 1 point or less (VGG16 bg_lo 67.2 versus 67.5, OHEM N=1 69.7 versus N=2 69.9, FRCN N=1 66.3 versus 66.3) cannot be separated from run-to-run noise using this paper, so they must not be cited as established effects or non-effects.
- The specific hyperparameters were tuned for FRCN on VOC and COCO: B=128, N=2, about 2000 RoIs per image, NMS IoU 0.7 on loss, bg_lo=0.1, the 1:3 ratio, and the learning rate schedules. They must not be carried over as validated settings for a small ember patch classifier or a temporal classifier.
- The claim that OHEM removes the need for foreground to background balancing rests on thousands of candidates per image on VOC and COCO, where foreground RoIs are available in most images (the latter is my inference, not a statement in the paper). Rare-positive regimes and small candidate pools were not tested. wfglink must not drop class balancing or a positive quota without its own ablation.
- The paper's statement that OHEM 'is helpful when dealing with smaller sized objects' is inferred from a +4.9 AP gain on COCO medium objects. Small-object AP only went from 3.5 to 5.0. Nothing in the paper concerns few-pixel point-like bright blobs, so it must not be cited as evidence that OHEM helps tiny ember candidates.
- The claim that effectiveness grows as datasets become larger and harder rests on VOC versus COCO comparisons. It says nothing about small student datasets of a few clips and must not be used to predict wfglink behaviour.
- Gains were not uniform across classes; some VOC07 classes got worse (aeroplane 74.6 to 71.2, sofa 68.7 to 66.2). Do not assume OHEM will improve rejection of every distractor type (headlights, reflections, sparks, lamps) evenly.
- The N=1 result covers correlation among RoIs within one still image, and the authors' own conclusion frames it as robustness when fewer images per batch are needed to save GPU memory. It is not evidence of robustness to frame-to-frame near-duplicates in video or to many patches from one track. Do not cite it for temporal correlation.
- The paper does not discuss label noise. Selecting the highest-loss examples may preferentially pick mislabeled or ambiguous patches, and the paper provides no evidence either way. Do not claim OHEM is robust to noisy ember labels.
- The case for online over offline mining is the cost of freezing a deep ConvNet across hundreds of thousands of SGD steps. The paper does not compare OHEM to classic offline bootstrapping. Do not claim online mining is shown to beat periodic offline hard-negative mining for a tiny, cheap-to-retrain classifier.
- The Table 2 timing and memory figures (Titan X, VGGM and VGG16), the 'more than 2 times faster' implementation claim and the unmeasured 'training is faster' claim versus B=2048 do not transfer to a small patch classifier or to runtime on a booth laptop. OHEM is described as a modification of SGD training, so by construction it should not change inference (my inference; the paper does not state inference cost), and the paper says nothing about inference-time verification latency.
- OHEM is a training-time sampling method. It provides no inference-time acceptance rule, threshold, calibration, temporal reasoning, tracking, or causal decision logic. It cannot justify any design choice in the rules stage or in track acceptance by itself.
- The finding that the bg_lo heuristic is suboptimal is specific to IoU-based background sampling in FRCN, and even there VGG16 was roughly unchanged without it (67.2 versus 67.5). Do not generalize it to 'all hand-made sampling heuristics hurt'. The 2016 state-of-the-art claims (78.9 and 76.3 mAP) also depend on multi-scale and iterative bbox regression and are irrelevant to wfglink.

Proposed adaptations (ours, not the authors' claims):

- Proposed adaptation (loss-ranked selection over the candidate pool): at each training step of the small patch classifier, sample a few training clips or frames, take ALL candidates the existing blob detector produced in them as the pool (the analogue of RoIs), run the classifier forward without gradients, compute per-candidate classification loss with no reduction, and backpropagate only the top-K highest-loss candidates. Decide and document whether top-K is taken per frame or over the pooled candidates, since the paper is not explicit. Because the model is tiny, the simple loss-masking implementation (paper option 1) is likely adequate; the two-copy trick is probably not needed (untested judgment).
- Proposed adaptation (spatial plus temporal deduplication): before taking the top K, apply loss-ranked NMS among candidates that overlap in the same frame, as the paper does with IoU 0.7 (the threshold must be retuned for small blobs). Add a temporal step the paper does not have: allow at most one or a few patches per track per short time window, because consecutive frames of one blob are near-duplicates and would double count loss, the same concern the paper raises for overlapping RoIs.
- Proposed adaptation (hybrid sampling with a positive floor): since ember positives may be rare and the paper never tested extreme rarity, keep a minimum quota of labelled ember positives and a small random slice of easy negatives in each minibatch, plus the hard-selected examples. Ablate this against pure OHEM (no ratio) and against a fixed-ratio random sampler before choosing.
- Proposed adaptation (add classic offline bootstrapping for precision): because retraining a tiny classifier is cheap, also run the verifier periodically over negative-only training footage (bright non-ember scenes such as lamps, headlights, reflections, glints) and add its accepted false positives to a persistent hard-negative bank sampled in later epochs. This is the Sung and Poggio style bootstrapping the paper describes in Section 1 (and the non-SVM variant in Section 2). It suits a precision-first goal. The paper did not compare the two approaches, so wfglink should test both.
- Proposed adaptation (leakage control): mine hard examples only from training videos, never from validation or sealed test videos. Split by video or recording event, not by frame, so mined near-duplicates cannot leak across splits.
- Proposed adaptation (sampling-independent monitoring, mirroring Section 5.5): evaluate each checkpoint on the full candidate set of a fixed validation split. Report mean loss per candidate together with precision-first metrics such as precision at a fixed recall and false accepted tracks per minute, so that methods with different samplers are compared fairly.
- Proposed adaptation (ablation design mirroring Table 1): compare (a) random sampling with a fixed class ratio, (b) a heuristic hard-negative sampler, for example candidates near the ember brightness or color thresholds, as the analogue of bg_lo, (c) loss-ranked OHEM with dedup, and (d) training on all candidates with a retuned learning rate. Use the same schedule, several seeds, and held-out videos, and report confidence intervals rather than single numbers (the paper itself reports only single runs).
- Proposed adaptation (label-noise guard, not from the paper): log the top-loss candidates every epoch for human audit, cap how often any single candidate or track can be selected, and allow annotators to mark ambiguous patches so they are excluded from hard selection. This keeps loss-based mining from amplifying labelling errors.
- Proposed adaptation (temporal classifier): apply the same loss-ranked selection at the level of track windows. Compute the loss per causal window of each track, select the hardest windows per minibatch, and deduplicate overlapping windows from the same track by temporal overlap, the analogue of IoU-based NMS.
- Proposed adaptation (precision-weighted loss for ranking): rank by classification loss only, since the patch classifier has no box regression term (the paper's ranking loss includes bbox regression). If the verifier must be precision-first, consider weighting the loss on false accepts (negatives scored as ember) more heavily when ranking hardness, and treat this as a hyperparameter to validate, not something the paper supports.
